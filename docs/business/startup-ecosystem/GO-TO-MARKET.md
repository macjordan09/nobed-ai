# Go-To-Market

How noBed.ai gets from a working demo to hospitals actually using it — and then scales.

---

## The core insight: sell into a role that already exists

The research found that some hospitals run a **"Bed Bureau Office"** — a unit that keeps a
roster of departments and counts available beds throughout the day to help decongest the
emergency department. It was the **2nd most-cited effective fix** in the study.

**We don't ask hospitals to adopt a new workflow. We digitise one they already trust — and
connect it across institutions.** That's the whole GTM wedge: lower adoption cost, existing
internal champion, immediate familiarity.

## Positioning for the sale

> *"noBed.ai is the digital Bed Bureau — connected across every hospital."*

Not "a new system to learn." A better version of a job someone in the building already does by
hand, plus visibility they've never had beyond their own walls.

---

## The three-step motion

### Step 1 — Land a beachhead region *(pilot)*
Pick **one region** and a **cluster** of facilities that already refer to each other:
- 1–2 receiving tertiary/regional hospitals (the destinations).
- Several referring district/municipal facilities (the senders).
- The local **NAS dispatch** point.

Why a cluster, not a single hospital: coordination value only appears **between** facilities.
One hospital on the map is a directory entry; a connected cluster is a working referral loop.

**Prove one number: time-to-placement.** Plus futile referrals avoided and stale-data rate.

### Step 2 — Expand region by region
Each new hospital makes the map more valuable to every other — the network effect compounds.
Move outward from the beachhead into adjacent facilities, then to the priority regions:
**Greater Accra, Ashanti, Central, Northern.**

### Step 3 — Go system-wide
With multi-region proof, sell the **national platform + analytics licence** to GHS/MoH. Now the
pitch changes from "help your ED" to "see and steer the whole system — and know where to put
the next ICU."

## Adoption levers

- **SMS-first** removes the connectivity barrier — no hospital is "too rural to participate."
- **Zero-cost entry for the public** builds demand-side pressure and awareness.
- **The data-trust layer** earns clinician trust — the hardest and most important sale. "Never
  a false green" is a promise clinicians will test, and we want them to.
- **Cheap to run** means a pilot doesn't need a fortune to stand up.

## What we need to de-risk (honest)

1. **Live data quality** — will hospitals keep capacity fresh? (Mitigation: SMS updates from
   the ward, stale-data alerts, the Bed Bureau champion.)
2. **Institutional buy-in speed** — public-health procurement is slow. (Mitigation: start with
   a willing cluster, prove the metric, then approach GHS/MoH with evidence.)
3. **The last-mile behaviour change** — dispatchers and staff actually checking the map.
   (Mitigation: make it faster than a phone call, and route it into existing workflows.)

---

## The first 90 days of a pilot (illustrative)

- **Weeks 1–3:** onboard the cluster, register facilities, train Bed Bureau champions.
- **Weeks 4–6:** go live with SMS updates + dispatcher use; baseline time-to-placement.
- **Weeks 7–12:** measure, tune the stale-data thresholds, capture case studies, report the
  metric to GHS/MoH.
